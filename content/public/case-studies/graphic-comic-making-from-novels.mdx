Mary Shelley wrote Chapter IV of _Frankenstein_ as one dense grey block of 1818 prose, yet every sentence is staged like cinema. I built `sk-comics` to revive public-domain classics into coherent graphic editions without losing the ink, type, or pulse of the original book.

## Ink, tooth, and five pigments

Every classic carries its own physical press signature before a single panel is drawn. For _Frankenstein_, the direction locks dip-pen gothic crosshatching on heavy paper tooth, deep focus with zero camera blur, and five exact hex pigments so night never drifts into glossy neon.

Each title gets a period-specific medium instead of one generic filter. _Paradise Lost_ takes 17th-century copperplate engraving with sulfurous spot lighting, while _Mrs. Dalloway_ switches to Bloomsbury gouache wash over cotton rag.

## Type inside the drawing

Unconstrained image models render trade covers like cinema posters and pack balloons with modern sans-serif fonts that break the period illusion. I locked book covers to period serif title proportioning and capped dialogue balloons at 12 words in a single Caslon serif hand so lettering belongs inside the ink linework.

Proper names and long speeches still trip pixel-level rendering when models guess letterforms. Setting a clear top-third sky or ceiling band in the layout keeps captions readable without painting speech tails across faces.

## Faces across sixty pages

One style anchor locks the book's line weight, grain, and balloon key, yet characters still drift between chapters if described only in prose. Every recurring figure receives a four-angle turnaround sheet and a 35-word physical lock covering coat cut, age, and scars before page generation begins.

## Cutting sentences into panels

Turning a chapter into sequential art means cutting summary prose into physical actions and choosing where time slows down. The press assigns every beat a panel weight so quiet atmosphere plays across wide tiers while turning points like the galvanic spark take a dominant Weight 4 frame.

## Inside the live press

On disk, `pages_manifest.json` plans every spread before Flora MCP renders twenty pages in parallel waves against the style and cast locks. Text-only TypeSafe Jev gates check beat coverage and source fidelity in one-second batches, while a vision pass transcribes rendered speech balloons for typo checks and final 12-up contact sheets get reviewed by eye.

## Five centuries on one shelf

Running one shared press across five centuries tests how far period art direction bends without losing clarity. When Percy Shelley's 14-line _Ozymandias_ stretched awkwardly into a 35-page narrative comic, scrapping sequential panels for a 14-plate steel-engraved visual essay taught the system when a text wants diagrams over speech balloons.

## Looking back

- Strict five-pigment locks and 12-word balloons trade happy accidents for whole-book coherence, and the pages read better for it.
- Baking lettering directly into artwork avoids pasted-on PDF text boxes, though faster image engines with higher typo rates still need a separate vector text pass.

Full circle: I ended up running OCR on Scott McCloud's _Understanding Comics_ to critique sequential pacing with the same rules that built the press.
